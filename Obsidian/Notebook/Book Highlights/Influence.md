## Contrast Principle
- when comparing two objects/ideas, humans tend to see the second object/idea as more/less than what it actually is.

- E.g.: Rob has to move two boxes, one is heavy and the other light, into the back of a truck. If he picks up the heavy one first, the light one will seem lighter than it actually is. The same applies if he picks up the light one before the heavy one.

## Reciprocity Rule
- after receiving a gift/favor, humans feel obliged to offer a gift in return so as to not feel indebt. The size of the return gift can sometimes be decided by the first party, if they are skillful with the rule.
- this rule also applies to concessions given from one party to the other.
- when a request is denied, if Rob makes a second smaller request, it is seen as a concession to Alice. Alice, feeling obligated to return the concession, offers the only thing she has at the time, her acceptance. This is used in the rejection-then-retreat technique.

## Reject-then-retreat Technique
- this technique plays off of both the contrast principle and the reciprocity rule.
- when making a request, it is wise to first make a larger -- more grandiose -- request that you have no intention of getting accepted. Then, for your second (or final) request, your actual goal can be requested, given that it seems subjectively smaller than the first request (even if it is objectively not as small as it seems).
- due to the contrast principle, the second request will seem smaller than the first and will seem more favorable. Additionally, having seen Rob's second request as a concession, Alice will feel obliged to offer a concession as well (acceptance).

## Consistency Rule
- what others think is true of us influences what we think is true.
- some small commitments can be used to change a person's self-image.
- this is not always the case.
- to affect self-image, certain conditions must be present: it must be written, it must be as public as possible, it must incite a feeling of choice or decision (without outside pressures).

## Social Proof
- we use the opinions of others to determine something for ourself.
- e.g. if someone sees a person laughing at a joke, they may then believe that the joke is funny.
- is extremely powerful when uncertainty is ubiquitous among the crowd.
- e.g. if an accident were to happen near a crowd, most people would look around at each other (without making it obvious, most times) to figure out what to do based on what others are doing.
- often times falsified by advertisers and for-profit companies to claim that a product/service is liked by a lot of people already so we should like that product/service.
